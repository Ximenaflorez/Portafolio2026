## 1. Initial problem

*Tension: why did this matter?*

**Business context:** A consumer-credit fintech wanted to test food credit in neighborhood stores. Stores are the preferred place to shop (35.3%), yet shopkeepers —70% of whom earn one minimum wage— are lending less and less.

**User problem:** Middle-class households (~2.95 people, earning 1 to 2 million pesos) allocate 31.52% of income to food, but the basic basket requires 47.30%. Cash does not last the month.

**How we knew:** A household-spend study (Oct. 2023) showed that 20% of spending already came from credit. Shopkeepers reported more need to offer store credit, and repayment was getting later.

**Constraints:** The business is relational (trust 27.1%, satisfaction 25%, loyalty 24.1%). Thin margins, and friction around charging interest on staple food.

**Goal and success metric:** Define the viability of two products —a digital tab and electronic store credit— with an extra cost the shopper can tolerate (~4.17%).

## 2. Research and data

*Discovery: what was not obvious?*

- **31.5%** of the budget allocated to food
- **47.3%** what the basic basket actually requires
- **20%** of household spending already financed with credit

**Methods:** Review of household spend and analysis of the traditional channel: store credit, margins, and competition with hard discount.

**Sample:** Middle-class households and traditional-channel shopkeepers.

**Findings:**

- The digital tab covers the month-end top-up when food prices rise.
- It stays near the 20% already financed, without exceeding repayment capacity.
- It opens a credit history for millennials and Gen Z who have no score.
- A word-of-mouth tab needs time and trust; displacement makes that harder.
- For the store, handing off the tab secures cash and removes default risk.

> **Insight that changed the course:** The product does not replace the basket: it comes in as a month-end top-up. The store adopts it if the risk moves to a third party that guarantees amount and term.

## 3. Solution architecture

*Decision: what did you choose and what did you drop?*

- **Digital tab:** $10,000 – $100,000\
  30 days · small, revolving purchases
- **Electronic store credit:** $150,000 – $1,000,000\
  60 days · a full minimarket shop

**Key decisions:** Two modes instead of one generic loan: ranges, terms, and reasons do not match. A single amount was dropped.

**System and components:** Reuse the existing lending system and add the new modes to the portfolio.

**Validation:** Desirability (31.52% vs 47.30%), feasibility (reuse of the system), and viability (~4% extra cost). *To complete: tests with people.*
